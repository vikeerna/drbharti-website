Understanding the condition

What is a hernia?

A hernia is a bulge that appears when fat or part of the bowel pushes through a weak area in the abdominal wall. Common types are inguinal and femoral hernias in the groin, umbilical hernias at the navel, and incisional hernias at an old operation scar. A hernia does not heal on its own; belts may ease discomfort but do not repair it.

Hernia symptoms

  • A swelling in the groin, at the navel or near an old scar
  • A bulge that becomes more obvious when standing, coughing or lifting and may go back when lying down
  • Heaviness, dragging discomfort or pain, especially at the end of the day
  • A hernia that has returned after an earlier repair

A hernia that becomes painful, firm and will not go back, especially with vomiting or difficulty passing stool or gas, needs urgent hospital care.

Where hernias occur

Types of hernia

Hernias are named by where they appear on the abdominal wall.

  • Inguinal hernia: in the groin; the most common type, especially in men
  • Femoral hernia: just below the groin crease, more common in women; has a higher risk of getting trapped, so repair is usually advised
  • Umbilical hernia: at the belly button; common after pregnancy or with excess weight
  • Ventral or epigastric hernia: in the midline between the breastbone and the navel
  • Incisional hernia: at the scar of a previous operation
  • Recurrent hernia: a hernia that has come back after an earlier repair
  • Lumbar hernia: an uncommon hernia at the side or back of the abdomen

How hernias are repaired

Laparoscopic hernia repair and abdominal wall reconstruction

TEP and TAPP: for groin hernias, the repair is done through three small keyholes from behind the abdominal wall. The hernia is reduced and a mesh is placed to cover the whole weak area; abdominal pressure then holds the mesh flat.

eTEP (extended-view totally extraperitoneal repair): the surgeon works in the space between the muscle layers without entering the abdominal cavity, which allows a large mesh to reinforce wide defects such as incisional hernias without mesh touching the bowel.

Rives–Stoppa and eTEP-RS: the mesh is placed behind the rectus (six-pack) muscles after the defect is closed; eTEP-RS does this through keyholes.

Abdominal wall reconstruction and TAR: for large, complex or recurrent incisional hernias, transversus abdominis release (TAR) frees a deep muscle layer so that the abdominal wall can be closed and a large mesh placed behind the muscles.

The first consultation

Which type of hernia do you have?

A hernia occurs when tissue pushes through a weak area. A bulge may become more obvious with coughing or standing. Groin, umbilical and incisional hernias need different assessments; examination helps identify the type and whether further tests are needed.

Medical names, explained simply

Conditions and operations you may hear about

Groin, navel and abdominal-wall hernias

Inguinal and femoral hernias occur in the groin. Umbilical hernias occur around the navel. Ventral hernia describes a defect in the abdominal wall; an incisional hernia develops at a previous surgical scar. Recurrent means a hernia has returned after repair. A lumbar hernia is an uncommon hernia at the side or back of the abdomen. Dr. Bharti’s practice at Samford includes femoral and recurrent hernia repair.

TEP and TAPP: keyhole groin hernia repair

Both approaches place mesh behind the weak groin area. TEP works outside the abdominal cavity; TAPP reaches that space through the abdominal cavity. The choice depends on the hernia, previous operations and assessment.

eTEP: creating more working space

Enhanced-view totally extraperitoneal repair uses a keyhole approach with a larger working space outside the abdominal cavity. The repair plan differs for groin and abdominal-wall hernias.

Rives–Stoppa and eTEP-RS

Rives–Stoppa is a repair that places mesh behind the rectus abdominal muscles. eTEP-RS applies those principles through a keyhole approach. Dr. Bharti regularly performs these repairs at Samford Super Speciality Hospital for suitable ventral or incisional hernias.

IPOM: mesh inside the abdominal cavity

Intraperitoneal onlay mesh repair places a suitable mesh inside the abdomen to cover the defect. It is a different mesh position from a repair behind the muscles.

Abdominal wall reconstruction and TAR

Large or complex defects may require reconstruction of the abdominal wall. Transversus abdominis release, or TAR, releases a deeper muscle layer to help bring the wall together and create space for mesh. Dr. Bharti performs TAR at Samford when the assessment supports it; it is not needed for every hernia.

The procedure is chosen after assessment. A technique listed here is not suitable for every patient.

An individual treatment plan

Options to discuss

Repair or observation?

Some adults with an inguinal hernia causing little or no trouble may discuss observation. This is not appropriate for every hernia. Symptoms, hernia type and your health influence the advice.

Open or laparoscopic repair?

Repair can be performed through an open incision or keyhole approach. Discuss why one approach is proposed for your hernia, including any previous repair.

Mesh and complex repairs

Mesh is commonly used to reinforce a repair, but the plan is individual. For a large or recurrent hernia, ask about the repair strategy, mesh-related concerns and expected recovery.

These are general options, not a recommendation for an individual patient. Your examination and reports guide the advice.

Planning ahead

Recovery and return to routine

Recovery after inguinal hernia repair commonly takes several weeks. Desk work may be possible earlier than heavy physical work. Ask for specific advice on lifting, exercise and driving; the schedule depends on the repair and your recovery.

  • Hospital stayPlanned laparoscopic hernia repair usually means going home the next day; large or complex repairs need longer.
  • WalkingWalking starts the same evening; stairs and light chores within about two days.
  • WorkDesk work after about 5–7 days.
  • LiftingAvoid lifting more than 5 kg and strenuous abdominal exercise for 4–6 weeks.

Informed decisions

Benefits and risks belong in the same conversation

Possible problems include wound infection, ongoing pain or numbness, recurrence, blood clots and injury to nearby structures. Ask which risks matter most for your proposed repair.

Lump in groin when standing — is it a hernia?

A groin swelling that appears on standing, straining or coughing and goes back when lying down is often an inguinal hernia. Other swellings can look similar, so an examination is needed. A hernia does not heal by itself.

How long does hernia surgery take?

A laparoscopic groin hernia repair usually takes about 45–90 minutes; complex abdominal-wall repairs take longer. After planned laparoscopic repair, most patients go home the next day.

Is laparoscopic hernia surgery better than open surgery?

Keyhole repair usually means smaller cuts, less pain and a quicker return to activity. Open repair or abdominal wall reconstruction may be better for some large, complex or recurrent hernias, and for some patients who cannot have general anaesthesia. The choice depends on your hernia and health.

Can a hernia come back after surgery?

Recurrence after modern mesh repair is uncommon but possible. Careful technique, healthy weight, stopping smoking and avoiding heavy lifting while healing reduce the risk. A recurrent hernia can be repaired again, often using a different approach.

What is umbilical hernia repair?

It closes the defect at the belly button, with a mesh when the size of the defect requires it. It can be done open or laparoscopically.

What is abdominal wall reconstruction (AWR)?

AWR is used for large, complex or recurrent abdominal-wall hernias. The muscle layers are released and moved so that the gap can be closed, and a reinforcing mesh is placed.

What is component separation or TAR?

Transversus abdominis release (TAR) is a type of component separation. It releases the innermost side muscle layer, creating space to close a large defect without tension and to place a large mesh behind the muscles. Dr. Bharti performs TAR at Samford Super Speciality Hospital when the assessment supports it.

When should I consult a surgeon for an abdominal bulge?

Arrange an assessment when you notice a new bulge. The surgeon can discuss whether and when repair is appropriate. A painful, firm bulge that does not go back, especially with vomiting, needs emergency hospital assessment.

Does a hernia go away without surgery?

No. A hernia does not heal by itself. Some small inguinal hernias with few symptoms can be watched after examination, but femoral hernias and painful hernias usually need repair.

When to seek urgent care

A hernia with sudden severe pain, vomiting, a firm or tender bulge that no longer goes back in, or difficulty passing stool or gas needs urgent hospital assessment. Do not wait for a routine booking.
